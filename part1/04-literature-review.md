# Literature Review

### Premature Data Extraction and Permission Coercion 
Recent research into digital loan applications highlights a widespread trend of aggressive data harvesting prior to establishing a formal user relationship. While general privacy literature often frames data extraction as a necessary trade-off for app functionality, studies on predatory financial platforms reveal that early data collection is frequently weaponised for hidden risk assessment. For instance, Akanji, Egele, and Stringhini (2026) and Pervez et al. (2026) observe that digital loan apps systematically bypass meaningful user consent by demanding sensitive system permissions, such as location data, before a user even registers. This practice stands in direct contrast to established software engineering standards, which advocate for context-driven permission requests tied to active features (Android Developers, 2026). Furthermore, the literature identifies manipulative permission loops where apps exploit system allowances to repeatedly prompt users upon unrelated actions, such as exiting a login screen. Rather than gracefully degrading the app experience, this mechanism converts hesitation into permission exhaustion, systematically eroding user agency before any financial terms are presented.

### Information Asymmetry and Deceptive Financial Design
A second major theme in the literature concerns the use of dark patterns to obscure the true cost of borrowing and trap users in asymmetrical agreements. While traditional e-commerce platforms might use deceptive UI/UX to drive engagement, digital lenders exploit these designs to mask financial liability. Research shows that predatory platforms frequently display borrowing costs as negligible daily rates rather than legally capped annual percentage rates (APR), delaying full disclosure until the application cannot be cancelled. This deliberate information asymmetry prevents vulnerable users, who are often urgently in need of cash, from making informed financial comparisons. Such design choices are not merely poor interfaces but calculated strategies to circumvent regulatory transparency requirements (Apple, 2026; Google, 2026), ensuring the full cost is only revealed once the borrower is locked in.

### Predatory Trust Mechanisms and Accountability Evasion
Finally, the literature underscores how digital lenders manipulate trust signals while actively evading accountability. Platforms often project legitimacy through superficial licensing claims on public-facing screens but construct barriers to verifying this information or contacting the lender directly. As Akanji, Egele, and Stringhini (2026) note, operational transparency in these apps is frequently concealed behind mandatory sign-in walls, such as restricting access exclusively to third-party services like WhatsApp. More alarmingly, the literature links the forced extraction of personal data, including emergency contacts, to aggressive debt-collection harassment later in the user journey. By hiding lender details and treating mere app usage as legal acknowledgement of broken privacy policies (e.g., routing users to dead data-processing domains), these systems create a facade of compliance while leaving borrowers entirely unprotected.
