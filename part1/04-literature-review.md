# Literature Review

### Premature Data Extraction and Permission Coercion 
Recent research into digital loan applications highlights a widespread trend of aggressive data harvesting prior to establishing a formal user relationship. Akanji, Egele, and Stringhini (2026) measured 435 Android loan apps across Indonesia, Kenya, Nigeria, Pakistan and the Philippines and found that 251 of the 342 approved apps (73.4%) violated Google's or national permission rules. In dynamic testing, 37 of the 148 apps that launched began transmitting sensitive data immediately on launch, 29 of them sending location data, before any registration had taken place. The authors also describe apps that request high-risk permissions at start-up and block further use if the user refuses. Pervez et al. (2026) show why such early requests rarely amount to meaningful consent: in their study of predatory financial apps, 85% of participants with limited literacy did not understand basic app permissions. This practice stands in direct contrast to established software engineering standards, which advocate for context-driven permission requests tied to active features (Android Developers, 2026). The literature, however, focuses on apps that block users after a refusal. It does not describe the softer pattern we observed in Credit Xpress, where a refusal is met with repeated prompts on unrelated actions, such as exiting a login screen. Rather than gracefully degrading the app experience, this mechanism converts hesitation into permission exhaustion, eroding user agency before any financial terms are presented.

### Information Asymmetry and Deceptive Financial Design
A second theme concerns the use of dark patterns to obscure the true cost of a product. Mathur et al. (2019) found dark patterns on around 11% of the 11,000 shopping websites they crawled, including designs that hide information until late in the purchase. Akanji, Egele, and Stringhini (2026) note that loan apps often obscure predatory financial terms such as excessive interest rates and short repayment windows, although their study does not measure how costs are displayed. We found no study that tests daily-rate framing in loan apps specifically. The closest evidence is from consumer research: Morwitz, Greenleaf and Johnson (1998) found that when a price is split into smaller parts, consumers recall a lower total and are more willing to buy. Presenting interest as a small daily rate rather than an annual percentage rate (APR) plausibly works in the same way, which is why both app stores require loan apps to disclose the APR (Apple, 2026; Google, 2026). If full disclosure is delayed until the application cannot be cancelled, this information asymmetry prevents vulnerable users, who are often urgently in need of cash, from making informed financial comparisons.

### Predatory Trust Mechanisms and Accountability Evasion
Finally, the literature shows how loan apps can harm people beyond the borrower. Akanji, Egele, and Stringhini (2026) link the harvesting of contact data in loan apps to debt-collection shaming, in which people in a borrower's contact list are targeted "despite never installing the app or agreeing to its terms", a harm they describe as "entirely non-consensual". The literature on consent explains why policies that treat use of a service as agreement offer little protection: Solove (2013) argues that individuals cannot realistically assess how their data will be used, and Obar and Oeldorf-Hirsch (2020) found that most people skip privacy policies entirely. We found no study that examines whether loan apps let users verify a lender's licence or contact it before sign-in. Our findings for Credit Xpress (an unverifiable licence claim, support behind a sign-in wall, and a privacy policy that treats mere app usage as acknowledgement while pointing to a dead data-processing domain) therefore extend this literature to the question of accountability.
